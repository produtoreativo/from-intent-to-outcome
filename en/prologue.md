# Prologue: The Word That Was Missing

I had twenty years of career behind me when I realized there was a central concept in my work that I had never named.

It was 2019. I was director of innovation and digital products at one of Brazil's largest retailers. The "Pick Up In Store" feature already existed, but there was a growing understanding that the model as it stood was hindering conversion: online shoppers were not completing the flow. I spoke with the business areas, primarily marketing and sales. There were hypotheses about how to deliver this feature differently to the user in order to improve conversion. What was needed was fast evidence, not a project.

I decided I needed no more than fifteen days to put something in customers' hands and test it. I assigned a strong frontend developer and an excellent UX/UI professional, handed them the challenge, explained the objective, and set the deadline. They delivered within the agreed timeframe. The UX designer even overdid it, in the best way: I received more prototypes than I would have had time to evaluate. I did not dampen the enthusiasm. The people who took part in the experiment were elated, with a sparkle in their eyes that I recognized: for the first time in a long while, they were working the way they had read about in the literature, doing what they had studied in books but rarely found room to do in their day-to-day corporate lives.

The problem was not building.

The biggest difficulty was convincing my peers, my boss, the surrounding teams, the company itself, that this was possible. The immediate response was direct: that's not how it's done. When I asked why, what I found revealed something I had not yet articulated with enough precision.

Nobody actually knew why.

The first answers came as questions: "but will it go through the GMUD?" Then came the appeal to Compliance. Then arguments about security. Each barrier I dismantled was replaced by another, all at the same level of substance: a vague sense that it was irregular, that some rule prohibited it, that it simply wasn't done that way. The discussion was proving more stressful than the development itself.

After much negotiation, we managed to run the experiment. Two weeks, as planned. We tested. We learned. We discarded what didn't work. What survived the test was forwarded to the traditional Discovery process, now with the acceptance criteria, the artifacts, and the ceremonies that everyone knew and trusted.

And then something I hadn't expected became clear.

The resistance I encountered during that experiment was not resistance to change, nor resistance to technology, nor corporate bureaucracy in the vague sense in which the term is usually used. It was something more precise. The people around me were operating within a set of commitments I was challenging without naming what I was doing. When I finally brought the result back to the process everyone recognized, the resistance vanished. Not because the result was different. Because the way of operating had become recognizable to them again.

The word that explains all of this is commitment.

Not in the sense of dedication or effort. In the sense of a specific type of agreement that changes what is expected of each person involved, what counts as success and what counts as failure. What I had done without realizing it, during those two weeks, was to operate in a mode of commitment that the organization was not accustomed to recognizing. When I brought the result back to the mode everyone recognized, the friction disappeared.

Hold on to that word.

It is the central spring of the Framework I began building that year, and which gives rise to this book.
